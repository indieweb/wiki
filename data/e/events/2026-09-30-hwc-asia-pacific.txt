==== Q: How do you deal with perfectionism? ====
* Want to participate, motivated by others, and the topic is set, but still feeling the resistance to begin and take the first step. Any tips?
* Naty: Had similar issues when starting, reluctant to put something out that wasn't perfect in her eyes. The heatmap widget helped her. At first, 100 words was the minimum to appear on the heatmap. This helped Naty get into the rhythm of writing.
** Has a separate [[notes]] post type, originally intended as a replacement for Mastodon ([[microblog]]). Eventually settled on ~300 to 500 words long which is a nice middle ground
** To Naty and Nadja, blog posts seem more serious, like a finished article; notes can just be a note on something
** Naty's Heatmap: https://burgeonlab.com/#blog-calendar
*** Naty blogged about it too: https://burgeonlab.com/blog/github-style-heatmap-calendar-widget-visualizing-hugo/
* Nadja: used to write professionally, has associations of something being more than showing up for one's self. Planning to experiment with notes.
** Challanges (carnivals) as skillbuilding: https://seeingteacupsindragons.tumblr.com/post/827298224619520000/i-know-that-its-not-important-to-all-writers-to
** You don't have to blog like me: https://danq.me/2026/07/02/you-dont-have-to-blog-like-me/
* James: Blogging is easy when writing about things that interest him, even if it's a poem of 5 words, it is worth posting
** You can always edit after the fact, it can be uncomfortable, but it gets easier with time, so don't let that from stopping you
* Sara: How do you start writing? How do you get an audience? How to get more social on the web? For carnivals, there will be at least one reader (the carnival host!)
** Plus you get the benefit of getting to know the host, which could be the start of meeting like-minded individuals
* Participating in carnivals can be a stepping stone to even more topics to write about
* [[the_void]]
* The first audience you should write for is yourself, first, and make sure what you're writing about is suitable for the public. You don't need to put pressure on yourself to write something that's ready to promote on Instagram, etc
* Places where you can share your website: [[directory]]
* "Show up for yourself first" —Nadja
** Changing the perspective from "creating for others" to learning to make things for oneself can be a difficult change as a majority have been conditioned by mainstream social media
* It's fun crafting your own story, style, length, with no limits when writing on your own site!
* Perfectionism gets better with time

==== Q: Do you have a publishing rhythm? How do you stay consistent? Are there any rules for yourself? ====
* Sara: Participate in all the carnivals! But no general rule otherwise
* If James pushes himself it doesn't work well, so he doesn't use a schedule
** Actually still feel tired from posting every day in August ([[Blaugust]])
** Keeping daily notes might help motivate or inspire writing blog posts, write what makes you happy
* Nadja writes a lot, privately in [[Obsidian]], but not sure how to choose what to put into blog? How to select which parts to share? When writing privately, there's no pressure, but when content will become public, checklists and the stress starts creeping in
** Obsidian feels safe, you can write a note with 50 different topics in one note, whatever makes sense to you, but for something that will be read by others, you become aware of how the note is structured, how it reads, does it makes sense, etc
** See what organically wants to become a post from notes, rather than forcing yourself to start with a blog post first
** The concept of a [[digital garden]] — the stages of notes (e.g. seed, bud, evergreen) helps remove the stress of having some finished product (evergreen) right from the start

==== Q: How do you manage your identity in public space? Who knows you have a site? ====
* Who knows about your personal website? (family, friends, etc.?)
* Not using real name, preferring to use a nickname
* Naty: sometimes tells friends that she has a website. But because main site is too technical so there isn't as much interest for her IRL friends
* Some people put their real names on their website, others have a separate professional site too
* Everyone is a bit different in terms of how they navigate their identity
* Want the personal site to encompass "me" which includes many sides
** Websites as a place to choose your own adventure: you can go to the more professional side or the more personal side on a site that has both
** You could make it a bit harder to get to your personal content if your site is primarily professional and you also want to write personal content (and you want your personal content to be a bit harder to find, but still there for friends / people who like exploring)
* Maintaining two personas can be difficult; there is a lot of overhead
** Thought about separating, with a site for each of the different personas
** Seeing how much we have changed when we look at our home page — noticing things that are no longer relevant or important to us
* Ryan already have an existing identity / online presence in open source work, but not sure if they want to link it to their future personal IndieWeb site
* James: rewritten bio and removed professional info, homepage is a poem, site is now personal, CV is there but unlinked
* Sara: uses real name on her site, always adding new aspects to homepage, haven't had to use personal site for finding work, so no pressure of being professional per se

==== Q: What's the most important thing to be on the homepage? ====
* It's exciting to keep bringing new parts of yourself on your home page
* Websites as a place to celebrate the different parts of you
** "Celebrate all the parts of you" instead of just showing one functional side to the world, e.g. professional side only
** The narrative online is only one part, want to be truthful and share the full person
** "Making space for different parts of me that exist. They are all me."
** "I am a whole person. I'm not a billboard."
* Websites as living creatures / places that change with time
** Personal websites are a window to who the author is
** It can take a lot of time to add all of your hobbies to a website — there's so much you can create!
** Naty has three domains, one for photography, one based on IG handle that is now being used for casual writing, and another for main website
*** In hindsight, a subdomain might have worked? photos.[domain name], etc. (it's too late now she feels!)
*** Subdomains are also free, unlike top-level domains
* James uses a domain that includes name + tld (.blog)
** Perhaps having name in domain means it will always be linked to the content
** The amount you want to share might change in the future
** Domains are semi-permanent, think before buying! Fixing by redirect can get messy
** Hindsight can make you realise you maybe want a different domain
* Real name domains means it will be used in professional work
** Having a personal/professional mix on your site can make you more self-conscious when you share your website for professional reasons
* If you move domains you will need to (or should!) keep your old one so that all your links stay alive, [[longevity]]
