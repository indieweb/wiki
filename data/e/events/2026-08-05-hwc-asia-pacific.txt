=== Rough Schedule ===
# Meeting warm-up (5 min) — Casual check-ins while people join
# Intros (2 min/person) — Everyone is encouraged to take part so we get to know one another! If you don't want to talk, you can type your intro here in the Etherpad and one of us can read it out. (Intros are mainly for when there are newcomers in the meeting.)
# Discussions — Everyone can add their list of ideas/topics/questions on the Etherpad, under the heading "Discussion Topics". We can do a quick vote to decide (if there's too many) and I'll save the rest for the next session if needs be.
# Group Photo — Midway in the meeting, we will take a group photo, i.e. screenshot. There will be ample warning!
# Demos — Before we round off the meeting, for ~10-15 min, anyone with something to show on their site can share their screens and do a quick demo.

=== In Comparison To Social Media ===
* Interactions between folks with personal websites vs folks on social media feels more nourishing
* Posting/interacting on socials feels more exhausting as there's more inputs/things to 'worry' about
** Chasing likes, doom-scrolling, playing the algorithm game, etc

=== Q: How do we share our blog posts without relying on walled gardens? ===
* Many pieces of software have grown by word of mouth
* Ideas don't and shouldn't have to grow via social media reliance
* Perhaps social media could be a potentially good bridge to encourage more folks to have personal sites?
** However there's risk of being influenced by 'vanity metrics' or being exposed to audience you'd prefer not to engage with
** Social media encourages 'mindless' engagement rather than thoughtful comments.
* Example scenario: Frustration after post being shared on Hacker News where a comments section opens to a wide audience about your work without your consent
* Choosing which channels we are on
** Which social networks we do / don't use, which search engines we allow to index vs. not
* Equally, people might share a post anyway without your consent (this is part of the web)

=== How To Define One’s Website ===
==== A website as a "canvas" ====
* Calling a site a ‘blog’ reduces it to a moment in time; something that needs to be updated
** It also sets up an expectation for ‘knowing what you’re talking about’ and ‘not changing your mind’
** Keeping your website’s definition loose makes it more freeing
* Folks new to creating personal websites believe they have to have blogs, because it’s so common, but it's not necessarily the case, websites come in all shapes and sizes!
* Social media has trained us to look for constant activity to ascertain if something’s worth paying attention to

==== A website as a snapshot in time ====
* However, writing things in chronological order can also provide memories to look back on
* Working on pages or collections is often more fun than a constant pressure to add more ‘content’
* Advice for what to put on a website?
** fLaMEd suggests starting with a single page. These are my interest. Come back next week. Take a bullet point on your list of things to make and put it on a page. Keep going. Soon enough, you'll have five pages, ten pages.
** Most of what Jo does on her website happens outside the blog; blogs are moments in time
** Blog posts can be snapshots of your life, but they have a weight to them
** Blogs as having weight; pages can be edited at any time without the expectation of there being an attached date
*** This is related to the idea of a "digital garden"
** Is there a broader expectation that a website must be a blog? Does this deter people from starting websites?
** Social media has trained people to look for activity / engagement, etc. But blogs don't have to be that way!
* fLaMEd enjoys working more on pages on the website than sitting down and writing a blog post
** Building a page is just as expressive as writing a blog post
** Naty often finds herself tinkering with her site instead of writing
* Designs that help you focus on the text, less clutter, a11y themes

=== Reasons To Have A Personal Site ===
* If we have our sites, we can have our own spaces without needing to rely on walled gardens
* A creative form of activism
* An expansive place to express oneself without monetary or commercial pressures
* A website can be almost anything: you get to make all the choices
* A website is an extension of yourself
* Moving away from social media gives you an opportunity to really ask what you want out of a home on the web: there are so many ways to do sites
** How the things we make might be an entry point or inspiration for someone to join the IndieWeb

=== Q: Why is it easier to write things for fun? ===
* Doing pieces for fun on your site feels easy compared to when there's pressure
** Writing for work or school has a certain weight to it
* However, if you write for a living, the perception of 'pressure' or 'obligation' lessens with time
* Things are often more motivating or intriguing if there's a community or someone else encourages us to learn

=== Q: What’s important on a personal site? ===
* Everyone has different focuses or priorities for their sites
* Jeremy wants to focus on a11y and content on a site, not interactivity/animations

=== Designing For Oneself Vs The Reader Experience ===
* Naty has noticed she's received feedback on her site's colour contrast which was not comfortable for a vision impaired person
** This reminds her it's important to design with the visitors' experience in mind
* Mediums and messages
** From the means by which you get text out there, or the means by which you share imagery
** HTML/CSS as a message
* Websites as free places to share different facets of yourself
** Information architecture reflecting what we want to focus on (poetry, essays, artwork, photography, etc.)
* Vibe of websites
* There’s a balance one can strike between a fun design and focus on the text: having a ‘reader-mode’ view
* Allowing readers to choose their own experience by offering different colours/themes
* IndieWeb is not explicitly about words; the IndieWeb is about self-expression and community!
* You can control styles, content, design, etc. on a personal website
** For example, https://chrisburnell.com/ has lots of different themes to decide from to fit the reader's preference
* Slow exploration
* 'Audience Potential' and 'Community Size' are the wrong things to look at when deciding to come into creating a personal website
** Other motivations make the experience more enjoyable
* Jo talked about navigation and not forcing people to go here or go there
** It's more about a vibe and the reader can explore the site in their own time
** Hope to discuss in a future HWC about navigation, because that is an area where odd pages in a site can be more visible without them becoming too cluttered
*** For example, a slot where a random link appears on a daily rotation
** If you change your mind, you can always write a new post and link in both directions
*** Jo likes the "On This Day" for that reason
* Rajiv learnt about [[note]] post type, and starting writing once a day on their new blank website, dietpy.com
* Digital gardens don't have dates, so it's different to blogs in that sense
** But do [[digital garden]] setups offer RSS? How can followers of a digital garden get updates since the notes are not dated?
** They won't have unique IDs as it's just the same note being updated continuously
** Could use a notice saying, x note has been changed recently or a simple changelog page whenever a note has been updated, added, changed, etc
* [[TiddlyWiki]] / FeatherWiki are [[wiki]] software that are alternatives to digital gardens

==== What To Use For Making Personal Sites ====
* Naty started using [[WordPress]], but got fed up after a while as it was not intuitive to learn the foundation (HTML/CSS)
** Jumped off the deep end by learning to use Hugo (a [[static site generator]]) and [[Git]]
** It's better because it's easier to understand and control, visually especially
** Also uses [[IndieKit]] (a Micropub server) for managing various types of her content
** Writes in [[Markdown]], for diagrams with Mermaid and D2Lang, and /stats page with Apache ECharts
** [[Micropub]] tools assist in posting to one's site more easily
* fLaMed uses [[Eleventy]] (aka 11ty, also a static site generator) Really likes it
** Writes in Markdown, and Eleventy transforms it into HTML once it's pushed to the web server
** It's good because you can see the HTML and CSS it produces and create templates for yourself
** Wouldn't recommend a static site generator for a beginner, best to start with handrolling/hand-coding so you learn the basics
** Once you need automation, try a static site tool
*** https://indieweb.org/manual_until_it_hurts
* Rajiv started with Hugo and NextJS, but with MDX
* Jo has a static site, and the blog section uses a heavily modified version of [[bashblog]], with a singular script that serves as a static site generator
** Has a 'category' feature where you can see how posts are filed
** Also uses a selection of bash scripts to do things like cross posting notes
** https://github.com/deathcomestotime/indieweb-bashblog
* Zach, the self-confessed silly, hand-types all his HTML. No CMS or SSG

** Never!! I'll retool my sites more often than actually writing anything...(I have three [[PHP]] scripts though.) :D
* James's setup is a patchwork: [[Ghost]] for posting, his custom SSG, and a series of Python scripts
** Could make it simpler, but doesn't want to go through the pain of doing so
* Static sites are more secure because there's less to update or maintain or 'hack' into.
** Jo's noticed folks try to get into their server via WordPress, but there's no point because there's no WordPress!
*** Perhaps have a fake WP Login page to infuriate the hackers!
* Many people use Bear Blog, which has a minimalist, text-based design
** It gives people a frictionless, simple entry point to the indie web with the simplicity of social media without the detracting elements

* Beedubyuh has experimented with many tools, but hasn't published anything
